Section~S1 describes the record, its provenance and the population, and repeats the main measurements on all handles. Sections~S2 to S4 follow the three choices an agent makes, where to write, how to sign and how to write: they cover the length of the feed, the classification of name pieces, the selection of conventions and the individual response curves. \section{Data provenance, task families and population}\label{sec:population}

\paragraph{Provenance.} Everything we know about the agents comes from the record and from the report that released it (ref.~5 of the paper, Von Arx et al., \texttt{collusion.wiki}, 4 September 2026). No transcript, prompt or model identity is available. The release contains 14,591 stored revisions of 4,579 pages, each with its timestamp, its username, the text added and the page after the edit. Two gaps remain: the histories omit edits below 64 characters on DSEWiki and the deleted pages that could not be recovered. Moreover, a username is not necessarily a single run.

The report establishes three facts that we rely on. First, the edits were made through plain read requests, which these wikis accept as edits, and 98.5\% of the edits on DSEWiki came from Microsoft Azure, the cloud infrastructure that OpenAI uses. Second, the task was a series of timed questions, usually five, on public statistics sites such as DataUSA, OECD and IHME, with a long first window and shorter later ones. Third, the agents signed with names they chose themselves. From the content of the pages, the report also infers that each handle was a sandboxed run that ended with its task. It does not state which model produced the edits, whether the task was part of training or of evaluation, or what the agents read beyond what they wrote: the server log of the release records saves, deletions and probes, not page reads. We assume what the report argues and no more. After excluding three accounts flagged as human and revisions without a username, 13,661 edits by 3,099 handles remain.

\paragraph{Task families and population.} A page belongs to a task family when its questions or its name identify one data-lookup task, such as the DataUSA clothing workforce task or the OECD Equity task; Table~\ref{tab:families} lists the 41 families. The population of the paper is the 1,201 handles that wrote at least once on a task page. Together they made 5,929 edits, 3,807 of them on 679 task pages. We assign a handle to the family of its first task-page edit; 945 handles wrote on one family, 168 on two and 88 on three or more. The largest family gathered 213 handles, and 795 of the 1,201 handles worked on at least one of the seven largest.

\paragraph{Spans and activity.} The activity span of a handle runs from its first to its last edit. It has a median of 2.02~h among the 827 handles with at least two edits (the 374 handles with one edit have no span), and this is the window within which a model agent makes its two edits (Section~S6). The distribution has a tail: 29\% of these spans exceed 6~h and 8.7\% a day, mostly because some runs renamed themselves and a few generic names were reused. A handle made a median of 3 edits, 31\% made one, and the most active made 176. Pages were written on for a median of 1.1~h, but stayed readable for a median of 17 days: of the 1,710 pages touched by the population, 1,692 were eventually deleted by the moderator, and 95\% were still readable one day after their creation.

\section{Reconstructed exposure and page choices}\label{sec:pages}

\paragraph{The feed.} Because the record stores every revision with its time, we can rebuild the RecentChanges listing at any instant: it is the list of the latest revisions across the wiki, by any handle, including handles outside the population. Which part of it an agent requested or read is not logged. We therefore take the last 100 revisions as the potentially visible feed, and the page being edited, as it was before the edit, as the potentially visible page. An \emph{arrival} is the first edit of a handle on a task page; of the 2,612 arrivals, 679 create a page and 1,933 append to an existing one.

\paragraph{The window.} How many lines of the feed an agent reads is not known, so Fig.~\ref{fig:pages_windows}a repeats the response curve of Fig.~\ref{fig2}a with windows of 15, 30, 100 and 300 lines. The share of appends that land on a page inside the window rises from 57\% at 15 lines to 72\% at 30, 89\% at 100 and 95\% at 300, and the fitted slope is 0.76, 0.86, 0.87 and 0.58. The two ends fail for different reasons. A short window truncates the choice set, so that many of the pages actually chosen are not candidates. A long window dilutes recency, so that a page holding a small share of 300 lines is chosen more often than its share and a page holding a large share less often. The response is closest to proportional between 30 and 100 lines, and at 100 lines most of the choices are inside the window.

\paragraph{The model window.} The model is just as robust to the length of the feed. Figure~\ref{fig:pages_windows}b repeats the model of Fig.~\ref{fig3}a with the agents reading the last 30, 100, 300 or 1,000 feed lines, and Table~\ref{tab:model_window} gives the tail of the distribution and the largest page. The body of the distribution hardly depends on the window, but the largest page grows with it, from 48 handles with 30 lines to 210 with 1,000, against 54 observed. With 100 lines, the 200 runs of Fig.~\ref{fig3}a give 0.030 for at least 20 handles, and the largest page has a median of 64 and a 5th to 95th percentile of 46 to 99.

\paragraph{What the proportional choice adds.} The model takes from the record only the number of agents and the number of pages each of them writes on, and its heavy tail comes from the choice of a line of the feed. To see this, we replace that rule. With a uniform choice among the distinct visible pages, the probability that a page gathers at least 10, 20 or 40 handles is 0.060, 0.003 and 0.000 and the largest page has 24 handles; with a uniform choice among all existing pages the figures are 0.068, 0.003 and 0.000, and 24 handles. The tail therefore requires that a page be chosen in proportion to the lines it holds, which is what Fig.~\ref{fig2}a measures.

\paragraph{The creation rate.} The model creates a page with a constant probability $p_c=679/2612=0.260$. Over the 2,612 arrivals this probability does not depend on how many pages of the same task were already active (Table~\ref{tab:creation}), and it lies between 0.23 and 0.33 on every day. The exception is the case in which no page of the task is active: these are 63 decisions in which an agent created a page 83\% of the time, because there was nothing to append to. A handle's first task page is created more often than its later ones, 0.34 against 0.20, and the model uses the pooled rate. Visibility matters as well. Of the 2,612 arrivals, 2,474 were taken with a page of the handle's own task visible in the last 100 lines, and 24\% of them created a page, while the 138 taken with none visible created one 59\% of the time. With two pages per agent the model makes about 630 pages (626 on average over 200 runs), against 679 in the record.

\paragraph{The agent's own task.} Agents prefer the pages of their own task. Of the 1,411 arrivals after a handle's first, 63\% fell on a page of the handle's own family. Among visible candidates, an own-task page holding 1\% of the last 100 lines is chosen 3.9\% of the time, against 0.5\% for a page of another task; at 2.3\% of the lines the two figures are 8.3\% and 1.0\%, at 5.7\% they are 20\% and 2.5\%, and at 13\% they are 35\% and 4.5\%. The response is proportional in both cases, with a slope about ten times larger for the agent's own task (Table~\ref{tab:reg_pages}). The model has a single task and ignores this preference.

\paragraph{Attachment.} A classic alternative to choosing from the feed is preferential attachment, in which a page attracts newcomers in proportion to the handles it already has. Figure~\ref{fig:pages_attachment}a measures this kernel. For every append to a page new to the handle, every task page edited in the previous 24~h is a candidate, and $A(k)$ is the probability that a candidate with $k$ handles already is the one chosen, over 317,679 candidate page-moments. Relative to a page with one handle, the rate is 2.0, 3.1, 4.9 and 6.7 at $k$ of 2, 3, about 4 and about 7, and then stays flat at about 6 up to $k$ of 25: a page with thirty handles is not chosen more often than a page with seven. Figure~\ref{fig:pages_attachment}b shows why, with the full cross-table among candidate pages inside the last 100 feed lines. At a fixed number of handles, the probability of being chosen rises about tenfold from one line to fifteen. At a fixed number of lines it barely moves: 0.06, 0.06, 0.06, 0.05 and 0.07 at three or four lines, for pages with 2--3, 4--6, 7--11, 12--24 and 25 or more handles. A page with many handles is chosen only while it is visible, and the kernel of panel a saturates because pages that have left the feed enter its denominator. The regression of Section~S5 confirms this: once the feed share is accounted for, the number of handles adds almost nothing.

(b) Probability that a candidate inside the last 100 feed lines is the page chosen, by the number of lines it holds (rows) and the number of handles already on it (columns); cells with fewer than 100 candidates are blank.}\label{fig:pages_attachment}

\paragraph{The branching approximation.} The Borel distribution of Fig.~\ref{fig3}a assumes that each feed line is picked by later edits a Poisson number of times with mean $1-p_c$. The model departs from this in three ways. A line is visible for 100 edits, and each of them picks it with probability at most $(1-p_c)/100$; the visits of an agent to distinct pages and the finite window make the picks dependent; and the distribution counts handles per page only because a model agent writes on a page once. The Borel law is therefore an approximation to the model, not an exact distribution of the record.

\section{Usernames and name features}\label{sec:names}

\paragraph{What a name is made of.} We split usernames into capitalized words and runs of uppercase letters; Table~\ref{tab:pieces} lists the 44 pieces carried by at least 15 handles. Thirteen are months, because June is written both as \texttt{Jun} and as \texttt{June}, and we pool them into a date stamp. We classify the other 31 by how concentrated they are in a single task: the share of a piece's carriers that belong to its most common task family, divided by that family's share of the population. The record leaves a gap in this number, with no piece between 3.2 and 5.4 (Table~\ref{tab:pieces}), and we cut inside it, at 4. The 16 pieces above the cut are specific to one task: words that name the task, such as \texttt{OECD} or \texttt{Grocery}, and roles coined on the pages of one task, such as \texttt{Watcher}. The 15 pieces below it are generic, such as \texttt{Open}, \texttt{AI}, \texttt{Agent}, \texttt{Research} and \texttt{Helper}. The generic pieces and the date stamp are the 16 name features of Fig.~\ref{fig2}b, Table~\ref{tab:reg} and Fig.~\ref{fig3}b.

Two generic pieces sit close to the cut. \texttt{OAI} (2.7) is mostly the suffix of the name patterns of two tasks, and \texttt{Scout} (2.8) was coined in the OECD task and then adopted outside it, by 35 to 55\% of the newcomers of other tasks in the blocks after 20 June. The classification does not drive the regressions: with all 31 pieces and the date stamp the coefficients of Table~\ref{tab:reg} are 0.11, 0.49, 0.20 and 0.04 (19,456 records), and with the 16 task-specific pieces alone 0.11, 0.51, 0.28 and $-0.04$. Task-specific pieces respond to the task-mates out of view, as a shared prompt would make them, but they still respond most to the task-mates in the feed.

\paragraph{The curve of Fig.~\ref{fig2}b.} Each record is a newcomer and one of the 16 features, with $y=1$ if the newcomer's name carries the feature. A newcomer enters if it has a complete 100-line feed with at least two earlier handles of its own task family, born in the previous six hours; $x$ is the share of these task-mates that carry the feature (15,776 records). The 16 features have very different base rates, so the curve is a component-plus-residual plot: we regress $y$ on $x$ with one intercept per feature, plot each record at $\bar y+\hat\beta(x-\bar x)+\hat\epsilon$, and average in bins of $x$ (0--0.1, 0.1--0.25, 0.25--0.45, 0.45--0.65, 0.65--0.85, 0.85--1, bins with at least 25 records), with a normal 95\% interval of the bin mean. The curve is therefore not the raw proportion of names carrying the feature. The raw proportions in the same bins are 0.04, 0.13, 0.30, 0.46, 0.69 and 0.79, against 0.07, 0.13, 0.26, 0.37, 0.53 and 0.57 adjusted. The raw curve is steeper because a feature that is common in a task is frequent both among the task-mates and in the newcomer's name, for reasons that the feature intercepts absorb.

\paragraph{The visibility test.} The regression of Table~\ref{tab:reg} splits the earlier handles into four groups (Section~S5). With predecessors of the previous six hours, the earlier task-mates on the page of the first edit carry $0.11 \pm 0.04$, the task-mates in the feed $0.44 \pm 0.04$ and the task-mates out of view $0.14 \pm 0.04$. At every window from 1.5 to 12~h the task-mates in the feed carry two to three times what the task-mates out of view carry, and the contrast between them is between 0.20 and 0.32 (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{ed:task}c; coefficients in Table~\ref{tab:reg_names}). The coefficient of the task-mates out of view grows from 0.14 to 0.22 at twelve hours, as the window takes in older task-mates whose names had more time to reach the newcomer through intermediaries. At no window does the page of the first edit carry more than the task-mates out of view: for names, the channel is the feed. A newcomer saw on average 11 task-mates and 34 handles of other tasks in the feed, and 12\% of the newcomers saw no task-mate.

\paragraph{What the test can and cannot resolve.} The test identifies the response pooled over 16 features and 41 tasks, not the response of each feature. For a single feature with task by three-hour intercepts the estimates are uninformative, $0.04 \pm 0.13$ for \texttt{Open} and $0.01 \pm 0.14$ for the date, because within one task and one block the share among visible task-mates hardly varies; for the same reason, the fully interacted specification is not identified for names. A coarser test needs no regression. We reduce each name to its skeleton, the order of its affiliation stamp, generic role piece, task-specific piece, month and number. A newcomer has the same skeleton as a task-mate in view 11.6\% of the time, as a task-mate out of view 9.3\% of the time, and as a visible handle of another task 4.0\% of the time; the difference between task-mates in and out of view is 0.023, with a 95\% interval of 0.011 to 0.034 over 555 newcomers. Most of the similarity between the names of a task thus comes from the task, and a small but measurable part from what the newcomer could see.

\paragraph{Who arrives first.} If newcomers copy what they see, the first arrivals of a task should leave a mark on its names. We take the tasks with at least 40 handles and the pieces that are not task-specific and are carried by at most 15\% of the handles outside the task: 273 pairs of a task and a piece. A piece carried by one of the first five arrivals of the task is later carried by 5.4\% of the task's handles, against 2.2\% for a piece that none of the first five carried. Part of this is not influence: a piece common in a task for any reason is also more likely to appear among its first five arrivals. A placebo that takes the last five arrivals and looks backwards gives a ratio of 1.4, against 2.5 for the first five, so the excess attributable to arriving first is less than twofold.

\section{Writing conventions and local choices}\label{sec:forms}

\paragraph{The candidates.} We produced the candidate list by having Claude Code read the added text of the corpus and list every pair of competing forms for the same thing, and completed it by hand. This is a search for variation, not a selection on outcome: the rule below is the only step that uses measured quantities. For each pair, regular expressions match the two forms in the text added by each edit, excluding links. An edit is a \emph{use} when it contains strictly more instances of one form than of the other (ties are discarded), and a handle's \emph{first use} of a convention is the first of its uses.

\paragraph{The selection.} Table~\ref{tab:conventions} lists the 52 pairs considered and why 36 were dropped. We keep a convention when the record allows its two prior probabilities to be measured: at least 100 uses, a minority form in at least 5\% of the uses, and at least ten pages with five or more uses. Thirteen candidates had fewer than 100 uses. For six the minority form never occurs, as for the 24-hour clock against am and pm. For sixteen of the other seventeen one form is rare or the uses sit on a few pages, so the prior strength cannot be told from the record: when every page shows the same form, an agent that copies the page and an agent that ignores it write the same thing. The last, \texttt{\&} against \texttt{and}, is a quotation rather than a choice: 177 of the 181 instances of \texttt{\&} sit inside a category name copied from a statistics site, such as ``Visual \& Performing Arts''. The profile-likelihood interval of $s=\mu_A+\mu_B$ in Table~\ref{tab:conventions} makes the selection explicit: its median width is 0.20 for the kept conventions and 0.69 for the dropped ones with both forms used. The kept conventions have between 274 and 2,878 uses, on between 11 and 166 pages, and prior strengths between 0.02 and 0.71.

\paragraph{Each convention separately.} Figure~\ref{fig:forms_each} shows, for every kept convention, the line of Eq.~\eqref{eq:copy} at its fitted prior probabilities against the records it is fitted on: every use with at least three instances in view, where what is in view is the earlier uses on the page, the writer's own included, or the uses in the feed when the page carries neither form (Section~S6). These records fill three to five of the five bins of every convention, and the line passes inside the 95\% interval of 67 of the 74 bins. The misses are \texttt{Rn}/\texttt{\#n} and \texttt{task clock}/\texttt{scaffold}, where uses on mixed pages take the first form more often than the line, and \texttt{update}/\texttt{edit} and \texttt{LIVE}/\texttt{live}, each at one or two intermediate bins. The intervals treat uses as independent, while a handle's uses repeat its own form (Table~\ref{tab:habit}), so they are too narrow.

The figure also draws, faint, each handle's first use alone, the records of Fig.~\ref{fig2}c. Over time, the coined names and near-synonyms go to one form and stay there, while the typographic habits stay split in every block of 50 newcomers from the first to the last.

\paragraph{Controls on the page.} Two controls separate the page from time and from the task. First, two uses on the same page could agree simply because agents born at the same time write the same way and land on the same pages. Figure~\ref{fig:patchwork}b therefore compares the observed agreement with 1,000 permutations of the forms within three-hour blocks, and within task family and three-hour block. Second, if the page works as a memory, it should predict a use even when read well before it. Table~\ref{tab:slopes} gives the response slopes with the page tally taken six hours before each first use: 0.90, 0.87 and 0.70 for the three classes, against 0.94, 0.79 and 0.85 at the time of the use. What an agent finds on a page is largely what was there six hours earlier, which is how a population with no memory of its own keeps a convention. With all uses in place of first uses the slopes are 0.95, 0.90 and 0.85.

\paragraph{The page against the rest of the task.} If each task family had a vocabulary of its own, the page would predict a form only because it belongs to the family. To separate the two, we reconstruct for every first use three shares of the form, each with at least three instances: on the page, among the last 30 uses of the convention by other handles on the \emph{other} pages of the same family, and among the last 30 uses anywhere. In the 204 first uses where the page majority and the family majority lie on opposite sides of one half, the handle follows the page 68\% of the time (95\% interval 61 to 74\%): 77\% for the coined names, 63\% for the near-synonyms and 67\% for the habits (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{ed:task}a). Majorities of exactly one half are excluded, and the 50\% reference is descriptive, because these conflicts are a selected subset of uses. Fitting the three shares jointly on the 1,798 first uses that have all three, with standard errors clustered by handle, the page carries $0.73 \pm 0.05$, the family's other pages $0.09 \pm 0.05$ and the feed $0.11 \pm 0.05$; the contrast page minus family is 0.63 (95\% interval 0.45 to 0.82; Extended Data Fig.~\ref{ed:task}b). The ordering holds in every class (page against family: coined names 0.86 against 0.05, near-synonyms 0.64 against 0.21, habits 0.79 against 0.08). Finally, with intercepts for every combination of convention, family and day, 434 strata, the slope of the response to the page share is $0.51 \pm 0.04$ against 0.87 without them: half of the raw association is between families and days, and half between pages of the same family on the same day.

For the 16 name features the first page matters less and the feed more. In a joint fit on 6,352 records the earlier same-task authors of the page carry $0.34 \pm 0.05$, the same-task handles of the previous six hours on other pages $0.22 \pm 0.06$ and the feed $0.49 \pm 0.07$. The contrast between the first two, 0.12 (95\% interval $-0.04$ to 0.28), is not significant, and when they disagree the newcomer follows the page 59\% of the time (n = 347).

\section{Identification regressions: samples, specifications, inference}\label{sec:regressions}

\paragraph{Samples.} The response curves of Fig.~\ref{fig2} and the regressions of Table~\ref{tab:reg} use different sets of records: a curve needs only the share it plots, while a regression needs every share it includes. Table~\ref{tab:samples} gives the unit, the eligibility rule and the size of each.

\input{tables/si_table_samples.tex}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Names and conventions.} For names, a record is a newcomer $i$ and a feature, with $y_i=1$ if its name carries the feature. For conventions, a record is a handle's first use of a convention, with $y_i=1$ if it takes form A. The outcome is thus the presence of the feature, or of form A, not the agreement with the exposure. The regression is Eq.~\eqref{eq:reg} of the Methods,
\[
y_i=\alpha_{k(i)}+\gamma_{\tau(i),b(i)}+\beta_{\mathrm{page}}\rho_i^{\mathrm{page}}+\beta_{\mathrm{feed}}\rho_i^{\mathrm{feed}}+\beta_{\mathrm{out}}\rho_i^{\mathrm{out}}+\beta_{\mathrm{other}}\rho_i^{\mathrm{other}}+\theta m_i+\epsilon_i,
\]
with $\alpha_k$ an intercept per feature or convention and $\gamma_{\tau,b}$ an intercept per task family and three-hour block of the newcomer's birth or of the use.

The four shares measure the exposure to four groups of earlier handles. For names, they are the fractions of handles carrying the feature among: the earlier same-task authors of the newcomer's first page ($\rho^{\mathrm{page}}$); the same-task handles in the last 100 feed lines ($\rho^{\mathrm{feed}}$); the same-task handles first observed in the previous six hours that are neither in the feed nor among the page's authors ($\rho^{\mathrm{out}}$); and the handles of other tasks in the feed ($\rho^{\mathrm{other}}$). For conventions, they are the fractions of uses taking form A among the corresponding groups: the earlier uses on the page, the uses by same-task handles in the last 100 feed lines, the uses by same-task handles in the previous six hours outside the page and the feed, and the uses by other tasks in the feed; a group can contain several uses by one handle. Each share requires at least two handles or uses. A newcomer whose first page has fewer than two earlier same-task authors, which is the case when it created the page, keeps its record with $\rho^{\mathrm{page}}=0$ and $m_i=1$, and $\theta$ is estimated and reported (Table~\ref{tab:reg_names}). For conventions no such indicator enters, because the record requires all four groups. For names a handle can appear both among the authors of a page and in the feed, so the page and feed shares are not disjoint; the out-of-view group is disjoint from both by construction. All these shares are potential exposure: the record shows what an agent could have read, not what it read. The coefficients are conditional associations, not probabilities that an agent copied.

\paragraph{Pages.} For pages, a record is a candidate page $j$ of an append $d$, with $y_{dj}=1$ for the page chosen, and the regression is Eq.~\eqref{eq:page_reg} with an intercept per append. The candidates are the task pages in the last 100 feed lines and the earlier pages of the handle's own task that are no longer in the feed. Each candidate appears once per append, and every regressor is computed from the revisions before the append. In 39 of the 1,933 appends the chosen page belongs to another task and is no longer in the feed, so that it is not among the candidates. These appends have no chosen alternative, and we leave them out rather than give them one, which leaves 87,349 candidates of 1,894 appends, each with exactly one chosen candidate. Leaving them out changes the coefficients of the linear model in the third decimal, and the conditional logit coefficients of the own-task and other-task feed shares from 9.93 and 8.29 to 9.17 and 9.13.

\paragraph{What each specification controls for.} For pages, the intercept per append already nests the task by three-hour effects, so there is a single specification. An own-task page that is visible is chosen 8.7\% of the time (n = 15,571), and one that has scrolled out of the last 100 lines 0.5\% of the time (n = 39,431). The number of handles already on a page, in logarithm, carries 0.006: once visibility is accounted for, audience adds almost nothing. The within-append $R^2$ of 0.10 is reported without a benchmark and is not a statement of fit.

For names, the main specification leaves one confounder open: a prompt that favours one feature in one task. Feature by task intercepts absorb it (third column of Table~\ref{tab:reg_names}). The levels of the same-task coefficients then fall together, to $0.03 \pm 0.04$ on the page, $0.15 \pm 0.05$ in the feed and $-0.04 \pm 0.05$ out of view, because within a task the three shares move with the task template; what identifies copying is their differences. The feed against the exposure out of view holds, 0.18 against 0.31 in the main specification (Table~\ref{tab:wald}, Extended Data Fig.~\ref{ed:task}b). With feature by block intercepts as well, which absorb a drift of a feature across the whole wiki and with it most of the variation in exposure, the contrast keeps its sign but is estimated imprecisely, 0.11 (95\% interval $-0.04$ to 0.27; fourth column). The page of the first edit does not differ from the task-mates out of view in any of these specifications. The share among other tasks is $0.08 \pm 0.05$ in the main specification and $0.19 \pm 0.06$ with feature by task intercepts: generic features such as a date stamp or a role word are also taken from the handles of other tasks in the feed.

For forms, the specification with one intercept for every combination of convention, task and three-hour block (third column of Table~\ref{tab:reg_forms}) is identified, because pages of the same task differ in the form they carry. It gives $0.68 \pm 0.07$ for the page, $0.07 \pm 0.07$ for the same-task uses in the feed, $-0.04 \pm 0.09$ for those out of view and $-0.08 \pm 0.11$ for other tasks; the page minus out of view is 0.72 (95\% interval 0.52 to 0.93), and the feed minus out of view 0.11 ($-0.12$ to 0.35; Extended Data Fig.~\ref{ed:task}b).

\paragraph{Other analysis choices.} Table~\ref{tab:choices} repeats the names and forms rows of Table~\ref{tab:reg} with one change at a time: a minimum of one or three instances for a share instead of two; task by time blocks of 1, 6 or 12~h instead of 3; and, for forms, the task of a use taken from its page instead of from the handle. The block length changes nothing. With a single instance a share can only be 0 or 1, and the noise of such a share lowers every coefficient (the page of forms to 0.56); with three instances the page of forms rises to 0.71 on fewer records. Taking the task of a use from its page lowers the page coefficient of forms to 0.56 and raises the exposure out of view to 0.06; the page still carries most. In every row the feed for names and the page for forms carry more than the exposure out of view.

\paragraph{Inference.} Table~\ref{tab:wald} tests the differences between coefficients directly, with the full covariance clustered by handle. Table~\ref{tab:twoway} gives standard errors clustered by handle and by task by three-hour block together (two-way clustering, not additional fixed effects): those of the page model double, those of names change by less than a fifth and those of forms by up to two fifths, and no conclusion changes. That the choice is close to proportional to the feed share is read from the curve of Fig.~\ref{fig2}a, not from these models.

\section{The copy model, the fit of its priors and its checks}\label{sec:model}

\paragraph{Implementation.} The model has 1,201 agents, the size of the population. They arrive at uniform random times over 5.3 days, the period that holds the central 90\% of the arrivals of the record, and each makes two edits on two different pages at uniform random times within 2.02~h of its arrival, the median span of the handles with at least two edits (Section~S1). At each edit the agent creates a page with probability $p_c=0.260$. Otherwise it picks uniformly one of the last 100 feed lines that point to a page it has not edited, and creates a page if there is none. Every edit puts a line at the top of the feed. On arrival, the agent decides each of the 16 name features by Eq.~\eqref{eq:copy}, with $\rho$ the share of distinct handles in the feed carrying the feature. At each edit it uses a convention with probability $u=0.230$, the mean over the conventions of the share of task edits that use them, and chooses its form by Eq.~\eqref{eq:copy}. Here $\rho$ is the share of A among the earlier uses on the page; on a page with no uses it is the share among the uses in the feed, and $\rho=1/2$ if there are none there either. The model keeps pages, names and the tallies of the forms. It generates no text and has no tasks, no quality, no reputation and no memory beyond the feed. Page sizes come from 200 runs, and every point of the prior-strength axis from 50 runs for each prior direction $\pi\in\{0.15, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.85\}$, with seeds fixed in \texttt{src/model.py}.

\paragraph{The fit of the priors.} For each name feature and convention, we fit $\mu_A=s\pi$ and $\mu_B=s(1-\pi)$ by maximum likelihood on the individual decisions with at least three instances in view, with $s$ and $\pi$ in $[0,1]$ and a grid of $\pi$ that is dense near 0 and 1. For a convention the exposure is that of the model: the earlier uses on the page, the writer's own included, or, on a page without any, the uses by other handles in the last 100 feed revisions. Intervals of $s$ are profile-likelihood intervals. When the fit puts one prior at zero, the direction $\pi$ is not identified. We then place the convention on the axis at the 95\% upper bound of that prior and leave it out of the binned record of Fig.~\ref{fig3}b. This happens for \texttt{Rn}/\texttt{\#n} and \texttt{deadline}/\texttt{horizon}; with them in the bins, the two lowest bins are 25.7 at $s=0.05$ and 5.5 at $s=0.15$, against 25.3 and 6.1 without them.

\paragraph{What the prior strength counts.} The model has no habit, but real handles do: a handle's later uses repeat its previous form more often than the fitted rule predicts from what is in view, in 15 of the 16 conventions (Table~\ref{tab:habit}). The fit on all uses counts this habit as copying. In the record, $s$ is therefore an effective quantity, the probability that a use follows neither what is in view nor the writer's own earlier choice, while in the model it is the probability of an independent choice. Fitted on first uses alone, where no habit is possible, $s$ is larger for 14 of the 16 conventions, and the drift from the prior is carried by the later uses: \texttt{seconds} is written in 89\% of the first uses of its convention and in 33\% of the later ones, and one handle alone wrote \texttt{sec} 159 times.

The fit on first uses needs its own normalization, because some handles write a convention many times. We normalize the distance by the effective number of writers, $N_\mathrm{eff}=(\sum_h n_h)^2/\sum_h n_h^2$, which ranges from 21 for \texttt{seconds}/\texttt{sec} to 452 for \texttt{Rn}/\texttt{\#n} and is a median 31\% of the uses. The record fitted on first uses then keeps the trend of the $1/s$ law but lies above it, at 6.6 at $s=0.24$ and 4.5 at $s=0.41$ against 4.1 and 2.5 (Fig.~\ref{fig:priorfit}a). This is not a quantitative confirmation of the law: $N_\mathrm{eff}$ corrects for the unequal number of uses per handle, not for every dependence between uses, and on first uses the prior of \texttt{Rn}/\texttt{\#n} goes to zero.

\paragraph{The fit and the outcome from the same uses.} The record places a convention at a prior fitted on the same uses whose final share it measures, which could by itself produce the agreement of Fig.~\ref{fig3}b. To test this, we put the model's own conventions through the same pipeline: 20 runs on the grid of Fig.~\ref{fig3}b, with the exposure, the fit, the treatment of a prior at zero and the selection rule of the record (at least 100 uses and a minority of 5\%; the model's pages are too short for the rule on pages), and the distance computed with the refitted $s$ and $\pi$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:priorfit}c). Where the prior is weak, the fit often puts one prior at zero, for 56 of the 100 conventions at $s=0.018$ and for none from $s=0.56$, and it overestimates $s$, by a median factor of 1.7 at $s=0.056$. Where the prior is strong, the refitted direction takes up part of the fluctuation of the final share, and the distance falls below the law. Binned at the refitted $s$, as the record is, the model lies between 0.68 and 1.26 times $1/s$ (0.85, 0.72, 0.82, 1.26, 0.91, 0.85, 0.70 and 0.68 from the lowest bin). The agreement of Fig.~\ref{fig3}b is therefore not produced by the fit. The lowest point of the record, \texttt{update}/\texttt{edit} at 0.03 and $s=0.71$, is a value that 3\% of the refitted model conventions of the same strength reach: at large $s$ the refitted direction is close to the final share.

\paragraph{The variance.} The Methods derive Eq.~\eqref{eq} (Eqs.~\eqref{eq:var} and \eqref{eq:distance}). The derivation treats the families of copies as independent, approximates the number of direct copies of a use as Poisson with mean $1-s$, so that $\mathbb E[T^2]=1/s^3$, and takes $M\simeq Ns$ families. It neglects the finite size of the population, the correlations between families that share a page, and the feed, and it holds approximately for $Ns$ large. We check it in the model on the quarter-decade grid of Fig.~\ref{fig3}b: the root mean square over runs and directions lies close to $1/s$ from $s\approx0.05$, and falls below it at smaller $s$, where few independent choices are made (24.9 against 56 at $s=0.018$ for a convention).

\paragraph{Strength, direction and the run.} Three statistics describe a run of the model and are compared with the record in Extended Data Fig.~\ref{ed:perconv}: the final share of form A; its \emph{volatility}, the standard deviation of the share over consecutive blocks of 50 first uses divided by the binomial standard deviation at the overall share; and the \emph{consistency within a page}, or co-occurrence ratio, the probability that two uses on the same page both take the minority form divided by the same probability for two uses on different pages. The strength $s$ sets how much these statistics depend on the run, and the direction $\pi$ sets which form wins. For a convention read from the page with $\pi=0.5$, twice the standard deviation of the final share across 50 runs is 1.0 at $s=0$, where every run ends at one form or the other, 0.80 at 0.02, 0.40 at 0.1, 0.14 at 0.3 and 0.09 or less above 0.4; it is about the same for a name feature. The volatility of the share over time depends on $s$ and hardly on $\pi$: averaged over the grid, the median volatility varies across directions with a standard deviation of 0.09, against 0.50 from run to run at a fixed direction. The consistency within a page falls with $s$ for every direction. Its level, however, depends on the direction, by about half the variation from run to run, because it is computed on the minority form; the model curves pool the five directions. Above $s=0.2$ the final share of form A equals $\pi$.

\paragraph{The usage rate and the window.} The usage rate sets how many uses a page accumulates, and it has little effect: at $s=0.2$, with the five directions pooled, the co-occurrence ratio is 2.4 with a rate of 0.5, 2.6 with 0.23 and 2.5 with 0.1, and the volatility 2.0, 2.2 and 2.3. Taking the last 30 uses of a convention anywhere, in place of the feed, as the fallback for a page without uses changes none of the outcomes at $s=0.2$ (co-occurrence 2.5 against 2.6, volatility 2.1 against 2.2). What cannot be removed is the fallback itself. If a page without uses starts from $\rho=1/2$, the pages become independent: the co-occurrence ratio falls to 1.7, the volatility to 1.2, and the final share no longer depends on the run.

\paragraph{Convention by convention.} Extended Data Fig.~\ref{ed:perconv} runs the model 50 times with each convention at its own fitted $\mu_A$ and $\mu_B$, and compares three statistics with the record. The consistency within a page has the right level, a geometric mean of 2.5 in the model against 2.7 observed, and is noisy convention by convention, with a correlation of 0.59 between the logarithms and 11 of the 16 observed values inside the 10th to 90th percentile of their runs; the coined name of a round, observed at 12, is the largest miss. The volatility is measured on first uses in both the record and the model, because real handles repeat their own form and the model does not: over all uses it is 3.3 on average in the record and 2.1 in the model, and on first uses 2.5 and 2.1, with a correlation of 0.86 and 13 of 15 observed values inside the band. The repetition does not drive the consistency: restricted to pairs of uses by different handles, the co-occurrence ratio of the record is 2.55, against 2.72 over all pairs. Finally, the final share follows the diagonal for the conventions with a prior; for those with $s$ below 0.1 the runs spread over most of the interval, and the observed share is one draw among them.

\section{Laboratory experiments and factual verification}\label{sec:lab}

This section documents the laboratory experiments and, in its second subsection, the analysis of the wrong answer in the record that they reproduce. The laboratory experiments of the main text use two models: gpt-oss-20b, served locally with vLLM 0.19.1 (OpenAI-compatible endpoint, low reasoning effort), and GPT-6.1 Sol through the OpenAI API (low reasoning effort; the Responses API for the experiments with a tool). Four further open-weight models, served in the same way on one GPU, repeat the experiments for Extended Data Figs.~\ref{ed:models_copying} and \ref{ed:models_checking}: Gemma 4 31B, Gemma 4 26B-A4B, Qwen3.8 27B (low reasoning effort) and Ministral 3 14B (Section~\ref{sec:lab_models}). Each trial is a fresh conversation, with no memory of other trials.

No trial is left out because its reply could not be read. Replies whose JSON is malformed are read by a tolerant parser, which takes the first complete JSON object of the reply or reads the answer field with a pattern. Replies that still cannot be read, or that ended in an error, are run again, and every failed attempt is kept beside the data. Table~\ref{tab:lab_design} lists every experiment with its cells, its countries and the agents prepared, missing and used. The prompts, the stimuli and every reply used are archived in \texttt{lab/} of the repository, and \texttt{src/si\_lab.py} recomputes the counts below from them.

\subsection{Copying (Fig.~\ref{fig2})}
The 820 stimuli are built from the record by \texttt{lab/stimuli.py} (seed 2) and come in four kinds. In 240 trials (P) the model sees a 100-line RecentChanges feed from the record and chooses a username and a page to open; the share of the feed lines of each page and the share of three name features (affiliation stamp, date stamp, \texttt{Agent}) among the handles of the feed are assigned at random. In 60 trials (P0) the feed has anonymous handles, so that there is nothing to copy for the name. In 440 trials (W) the model sees the feed and a real task page and writes the text it appends; every instance of each convention on the page is rewritten so that form A holds a random share, and half of the trials show conventions that agents used rarely. In 80 trials (W0) the page is empty. To keep the prompt itself from favouring a form, clock and number formats in the task state are drawn at random for each trial, the prompt has no key that names a convention, and name features are removed from page names in the feed. The system message describes the setting of the record (a timed task of five questions, a sandbox shut down at the end, other agents on the same tasks, a public wiki with no index and its RecentChanges listing), and the model replies with a JSON object.

Every model has a readable reply to all 820 trials, and two runs needed care. GPT-6.1 Sol first answered 767 trials, because its run stopped at its budget, and the 53 remaining W0 trials were run afterwards with the same settings. Ministral 3 14B wrote malformed JSON in about half of its free-format replies, mostly in the longer writing trials. Running only those again would have resampled a selected subset, so its 820 trials were run in full with the output constrained to a JSON object and the same prompt; its free-format run is kept in the repository.

Replies are scored with the definitions of the record: the page chosen against its share of the feed (146 choices of an existing page for gpt-oss-20b, 240 for Sol), each of the 16 name features against its share among the handles of the feed (240 names per model), and the form of each use of the 8 conventions whose share was assigned against its share on the page (450 uses in 296 notes for gpt-oss-20b, 526 in 339 for Sol). Slopes are least-squares fits on the unbinned records, with errors clustered by trial. In Fig.~\ref{fig2} the laboratory curves are drawn at the bin positions of the record by interpolating between their own bins; the interpolation is for display only and does not enter the slopes.

\subsection{The OECD answers in the record}
The OECD Equity task asked, for one country, the share of private expenditure in pre-primary education in 2018, to two decimal places. The source is a dashboard whose workbook stores the values with all their digits but displays them rounded to one decimal. Five questions, one per country, carry two competing values in the record (Table~\ref{tab:oecd_values}): the displayed value padded with a zero, which is wrong, and the right value. We match both in the text added by each edit, excluding matches inside longer numbers and in unrelated numerical contexts, with the machinery of the conventions: an edit is a use of the value that occurs more often in it as an answer, and ties are discarded. This gives 325 uses on 97 pages by 125 handles. The first note giving a padded value was followed by 159 further uses of padded values, by 79 handles on 55 pages, before the first right value with its evidence (20 June 2026, 03:41:17 UTC).

\subsection{Agreement and checking (Fig.~\ref{fig4}a)}
Each agent receives the question for one country, a wiki page of notes and one tool, \texttt{fetch}. The dashboard URL returns a short page saying that the report needs JavaScript and giving the URL of the Excel workbook; the workbook URL returns the rows of the indicator with the values as stored; any other host fails. An agent \emph{checks} if the tool log shows a fetch of the workbook; a fetch of the dashboard alone does not count. An answer is right or wrong if the number in it equals the right or the padded value; other numbers are neither. Agents have up to eight model turns.

The pages hold 0, 1, 2, 3, 7, 11, 19 or 32 notes giving the padded value of the question, drawn without replacement and in random order from the notes of the record written before the first correction that contain that value: 153 notes in all, of which 85, 109, 59 and 59 contain the value of the Czech Republic, Hungary, Poland and the Slovak Republic (a note can give several countries). Slovenia, with 15, cannot fill a page of 32 and is left out. Each cell has 25 agents per country for four countries, 100 per point and per model, with no missing replies (Table~\ref{tab:lab_agreement}). Of the 567 gpt-oss-20b agents that checked, one answered the padded value, and 8 answered right without checking; GPT-6.1 Sol answered the padded value after checking 7 times out of 408. For every country, gpt-oss-20b checks less often with 7 or more agreeing notes than with one (Table~\ref{tab:lab_country}). Laboratory and record measure different things, a fetch in the tool log against a citation in the note, and Fig.~\ref{fig:checking_record} keeps them apart.

\subsection{Agreement against correctness (Fig.~\ref{fig4}b)}
Each agent sees a page of 20 notes of the record and the question for one of the four countries of Fig.~\ref{fig4}a. The notes are drawn from the record notes that give the padded value of the question, leaving out the few that also mention the right value or the stored digits (5 of the 85 notes of the Czech Republic, and 1 or 2 for the other countries), so that no note contradicts itself once changed. A fraction 0, 0.1, \ldots, 1 of the 20 notes is turned right by writing the right value in place of the padded one. Right and wrong notes are therefore the same texts, with the same claims of provenance, and differ only in the number. There are 25 agents per country and fraction, 100 per point and model and 1,100 per model, all with an answer.

The two models respond differently to the composition of the page. GPT-6.1 Sol checks on mixed pages and almost never on unanimous ones: on pages with 30 to 70\% right notes its probability of checking is 0.92, against 0.02 on unanimous pages (difference $+0.90$, 95\% bootstrap interval $+0.86$ to $+0.93$), for every country (Table~\ref{tab:lab_country}). In contrast, gpt-oss-20b checks about half the time whatever the composition (0.56 against 0.58, difference $-0.02$, $-0.10$ to $+0.06$). When it does not check, its answer follows the majority of the page: right 0\%, 44\% and 90\% of the time on pages with 10\%, 50\% and 100\% right notes. A repeat of the gpt-oss-20b run on the same pages gives the same flat curve (Section~\ref{sec:lab_oss}).

\subsection{Synthetic notes}\label{sec:lab_synthetic}
To separate agreement from what the notes say, we repeat the experiments with short synthetic notes in place of the notes of the record (Fig.~\ref{fig:lab_synthetic}). Each note takes one of 20 template wordings, such as ``CONFIRMED: Hungary 9.90\%. Will relay if anything changes.'', and a random handle in the style of the record, and the order of the notes is shuffled for every agent. None of the wordings names a source, whereas 61\% of the notes of the record claim one or claim a check (``Dashboard workbook definitive value'', ``verified'', ``extracted from the xlsx''). Pages of $N=1$ to 32 template notes all give the padded value (design P, four countries, 100 agents per point), and pages of 20 template notes give a fraction of right values (design B: five countries, the four above and Slovenia, 125 agents per point, and for GPT-6.1 Sol the fractions 0, 0.2, \ldots, 1 only). For gpt-oss-20b the mixed pages also come in other lengths (5, 10, 20 or 40 notes).

Only gpt-oss-20b is misled by synthetic notes. Its checking falls from 0.92 with one note to 0.62 with 32, and on pages of 5, 10, 20 and 40 notes it is higher on mixed pages than on unanimous ones by 0.12 ($+0.06$ to $+0.17$), 0.22 ($+0.16$ to $+0.29$), 0.22 ($+0.16$ to $+0.29$) and 0.28 ($+0.20$ to $+0.35$). The other five models, GPT-6.1 Sol included, open the source in 95 to 100\% of the trials on every synthetic page and answer right in 91 to 100\%. GPT-6.1 Sol is therefore moved not by the repetition of a value but by the agreement of notes that claim to have checked it. When it answers the padded value without checking on pages of the record, 42\% of its notes present the workbook as the source of the value and 35\% say that they rely on the earlier notes (``consistent with the workbook value recorded in prior notes''), while 1\% say that the value was not verified.

\subsection{What drives checking in gpt-oss-20b}\label{sec:lab_oss}
In Fig.~\ref{fig4}a a page with more agreeing notes is also a longer page. To separate length from agreement, we use pages of $N=2$, 4, 8, 12, 20 and 32 notes of the record, half of them turned right as in Fig.~\ref{fig4}b (design M, 100 agents per point; Fig.~\ref{fig:lab_oss}a). Checking falls with the length of the page in the same way whether the notes agree or not: 0.90, 0.81, 0.66, 0.60, 0.53 and 0.43 on half-right pages, against 0.90, 0.81, 0.59, 0.55, 0.54 and 0.41 on all-wrong pages of 1, 3, 7, 11, 19 and 32 notes. For gpt-oss-20b, the decline of Fig.~\ref{fig4}a is therefore a decline with the amount of material on the page, and agreement matters only when it is easy to see, on short template notes (Section~\ref{sec:lab_synthetic}). Two controls confirm the flat curve of Fig.~\ref{fig4}b. A repeat of the run on the same pages gives the same curve (0.54 checking overall), and the same pages at medium reasoning effort are checked 0.93 of the time at every composition (Fig.~\ref{fig:lab_oss}b): more reasoning makes the model check more, but not in response to disagreement. Finally, a repeat of Fig.~\ref{fig4}a on the same pages and seeds reproduces the decline (0.30 checking with 32 notes, against 0.41; Fig.~\ref{fig:lab_oss}c).

\subsection{Chains (Fig.~\ref{fig4}c)}
A chain is one page and 30 agents that come one at a time. Each agent reads the page as the earlier agents left it, may fetch the source, answers, and its note is appended to the page as written; the agents share nothing else. The page starts with 1, 3, 7 or 32 notes of the record giving the padded value (for gpt-oss-20b also 19), sampled from the same pools as above and seeded by chain. There are five chains per country and starting page for four countries, so 20 chains and 600 agents per starting page and model, with no missing replies (Table~\ref{tab:lab_chains}). The unit of replication is the chain: the 95\% band of Fig.~\ref{fig4}c is $\pm1.96$ standard errors across the 20 chains of the share of wrong answers at each position, smoothed over three positions, and the intervals of Table~\ref{tab:lab_chains} are bootstrap intervals over chains.

Without smoothing, gpt-oss-20b agents 11 to 30 answer wrong 5\%, 5\%, 7\%, 25\% and 46\% of the time for 1, 3, 7, 19 and 32 starting notes, and GPT-6.1 Sol agents 0\%, 10\%, 90\% and 100\% of the time for 1, 3, 7 and 32. The chains of GPT-6.1 Sol are decided by their first agents: of the 20 chains starting from 7 notes, 18 give the wrong value at every one of the 30 positions and 2 the right value at every later position, and agents 11 to 30 open the source in none of the chains starting from 7 or 32 notes (Fig.~\ref{fig:lab_chains}). A chain that stays wrong also produces notes that claim a check: 57\% of the notes written by Sol agents that gave the padded value without opening the source name the workbook, the dashboard or a verification.

\subsection{Limits}
The laboratory models are not the models that wrote the wiki, which the record does not identify, and the laboratory tasks are single questions with one tool and a page, simpler than the timed tasks of the record. The laboratory therefore shows that the mechanisms measured in the record exist in current models under controlled exposure, not that the agents of the record had the same priors or checking rates. The notes of the record come from one task and one wrong value, so they carry one kind of claim of provenance; how other kinds of wrong consensus would fare is not tested. Turning a note right keeps its claim of provenance. This makes the comparison of compositions fair, but it also means that on mixed pages both sides claim to have checked. Finally, GPT-6.1 Sol ran the synthetic mixed pages at six fractions of right notes, not eleven.
